Breaking Down an Embroidery Price Quote

Figuring out how much to charge for embroidery requires separating a job into four distinct parts: the stitch-count decoration charge, the per-piece hooping and handling labor, any blank garment cost with markup, and one-time setup or digitizing work. When those four pieces are combined with a minimum per-piece floor and structured quantity breaks, you can quote everything from a single personalized jacket to a 144-piece corporate order using one consistent formula.

Every number in the walkthrough below uses the calculator's editable starting values ($1.50 per 1,000 stitches, $1.00 per-piece fee, $5.00 minimum decoration charge, 6 thread colors included, and $0.50 per extra color). These starting values are placeholders rather than industry averages—you should adjust them in the rate panel above to match your shop's overhead and target hourly rate.

Step 1: Calculate the Base Decoration Price

Decoration pricing starts with the normal stitch count of the design. Larger, denser designs keep the embroidery head running longer and consume more thread and backing, so the base price scales per thousand stitches. On top of the stitch charge, every garment requires physical handling: hooping with stabilizer, loading onto the machine arms, trimming backing after the run, and folding.

The base decoration formula used by the calculator is:

base = (stitches ÷ 1,000) × rate + piece fee + extra-color fee × max(0, colors − colors included)

Consider Worked Example 1: a local business brings in 24 caps to be embroidered with an 8,000-stitch logo that uses 3 thread colors (which means 2 color changes). Because the customer supplies the caps and provides a ready-to-sew file with no setup fee, we start with the base decoration calculation:

  • Stitch charge: (8,000 ÷ 1,000) × $1.50 = 8 × $1.50 = $12.00
  • Per-piece handling fee: $1.00
  • Extra-color fee: With 3 colors and 6 colors included in the base rate, max(0, 3 − 6) = 0 extra colors, adding $0.00.
  • Base decoration price: $12.00 + $1.00 + $0.00 = $13.00 per piece before quantity discounts.

Step 2: Apply Quantity Breaks and Enforce Your Minimum

Running 24 identical caps on a machine is more efficient per piece than setting up thread cones and hooping fixtures for a single piece. Quantity breaks reward larger orders while keeping small runs profitable. In the starting rate table, the quantity tiers are:

  • 1–11 pieces: 0% discount
  • 12–23 pieces: 5% discount
  • 24–47 pieces: 10% discount
  • 48–143 pieces: 15% discount
  • 144+ pieces: 20% discount

After applying the break discount, the calculator compares the discounted figure against your per-piece minimum:

decoration = max(base × (1 − break discount), minimum)

For our 24-cap order in Worked Example 1, quantity 24 falls into the 24–47 tier with a 10% discount:

  • Discounted decoration: $13.00 × (1 − 0.10) = $13.00 × 0.90 = $11.70
  • Minimum check: Because $11.70 is above the $5.00 minimum per piece, the decoration price stays $11.70.

Why does the minimum matter? Suppose a customer orders 48 pieces of a tiny 3,000-stitch monogram. At starting rates, the base price is 3 × $1.50 + $1.00 = $5.50. A 15% discount at 48 pieces would drop the raw calculation to $4.68, which is below the $5.00 minimum floor. The minimum rule automatically holds the decoration price at $5.00 per piece so volume discounts never erode your baseline handling cost.

Step 3: Round to the Cent and Multiply by Quantity

When no blank garment or setup fee is included, the per-piece price equals the decoration price of $11.70. Multiplying by 24 caps gives the Worked Example 1 order total:

order total = $11.70 × 24 = $280.80

Notice how the calculator rounds the per-piece price to the nearest cent (half up) before multiplying by the order quantity. If a 12-piece order has a 7,500-stitch design (base $12.25) with a 5% discount, the unrounded discounted number is $11.6375. Rounding half up to the cent gives $11.64 per piece, and multiplying $11.64 × 12 gives $139.68. That way, when a customer checks your quote on a pocket calculator, unit price × quantity always matches the total to the exact cent.

Step 4: Check Machine Time and Effective Hourly Rate

A per-piece price only makes sense if it pays enough for the time your embroidery machine spends running the order. Even if a machine is rated at a high maximum speed on flat test fabric, real jobs pause for trims, slow down on wide satin columns, wait during needle color changes, and require manual hooping between runs.

The calculator estimates total minutes per piece using three inputs:

minutes per piece = (stitches ÷ machine speed) + color changes × (change seconds ÷ 60) + hooping minutes

Plugging in Worked Example 1 (8,000 stitches at 650 stitches per minute, 2 color changes at 30 seconds each, and 1 minute of hooping):

  • Stitching time: 8,000 ÷ 650 = 12.31 minutes
  • Color change time: 2 × (30 ÷ 60) = 2 × 0.5 = 1.00 minute
  • Hooping and handling time: 1.00 minute
  • Total time per piece: 12.31 + 1.00 + 1.00 = 14.31 minutes per piece
  • Total order machine time (1 head): 14.31 × 24 ÷ 1 = 343.38 minutes (about 5 hours and 43 minutes)

From the decoration price and the minutes per piece, the calculator reports your effective hourly rate:

effective hourly rate = ($11.70 ÷ 14.31) × 60 = $49.06 per hour

Seeing $49.06/hr next to $11.70/pc immediately tells you whether the job hits your shop's revenue target. If your target is $60.00/hr on a single-head machine, you can open the rate settings above and raise either your rate per 1,000 stitches or your per-piece hooping fee until the effective hourly rate reaches your goal.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I charge differently when the customer brings their own garments?

When a customer supplies their own items, you set the blank garment cost to $0.00 and charge strictly for decoration plus any setup fee. Many shops increase their per-piece handling fee on customer-supplied items to account for unbagging and inspection time.

How do extra thread colors affect the price?

Each color change requires the machine to trim, move the pantograph to the next needle bar, and tie in the new thread. Setting an included color count (such as 6 colors) and adding a per-piece fee for each additional color covers that extra setup and run time.

When should I add a one-time setup fee?

Add a one-time setup fee whenever an order requires digitizing artwork into a stitch file, hooping test sew-outs, or loading specialty thread cones for a small run.
